# COALITION

## Service blueprint and checkpoint concept report

**Concept:** Ron Bronson  
**Analysis:** ChatGPT  
**Date:** October 29, 2026  
**Status:** Concept development, before implementation.

COALITION concerns the work of helping or blocking a person as they move through a sequence of ordinary administrative encounters. The current design question is how that work becomes interesting to perform. Its locations, politics, institutions, travel rules, and documents are available for invention.

This report accompanies the [origin and concept report](coalition-origin-and-concept-report.md), which preserves the earlier discussion, and the [interaction design sprint report](interaction-design-game-sprint-concept-report.md), which considers the related teaching idea. It records the revised player perspective and evaluates the interactions, possible cards, NPCs, and use of a service blueprint.

## Creative scope

Ron clarified the intended scope after the discussion became too focused on matching the source article and existing world material:

> my game can and should use entirely fictional details including making new nelosphere cities if i want

That clarification governs this report. Ron can invent cities, airports, routes, governments, laws, elections, agency powers, documents, and events. He can simplify travel, combine institutional functions, and place the final encounter wherever the game needs it. Existing Nelosphere material supplies optional context and texture. It does not limit the game’s authorship. [3]

The article contributes an observation: even someone with considerable power depends on other people doing particular work. A service blueprint can carry that observation into a wholly invented system. The game’s own relationships and consequences are what the player needs to understand.

The useful standard is internal consistency. An invented office can possess unusual powers if the fiction establishes what those powers mean. A new airport can be the entire story’s destination. The evaluation concerns whether the player understands the situation, makes meaningful judgments, and can interpret what follows.

## Where the idea came from

The supplied Wall Street Journal account reports that Flávio Bolsonaro left Brazil after an election defeat and a court restriction, crossed into Argentina, and traveled by chartered aircraft through Ecuador to Miami. It describes assistance from political allies, Argentine officials, and a commercial operator, followed by arrangements for U.S. entry and a temporary stay. [1]

Ron’s interest was the effort required to assemble those contributions. Money and influence did not perform the journey by themselves. Different people had to arrange, receive, process, transport, and admit the travelers. This suggested both a service blueprint and a game about navigating dependencies. [2]

Those details explain the concept’s provenance. They do not determine its geography, characters, documents, or ending. Forged credentials, invented checkpoints, a different political structure, or a compressed journey can all belong to COALITION’s fiction.

The original blueprint supplied a particularly useful unit: the handoff. An action in one encounter changes what someone can do in the next. That structure remains available even when every place, institution, and rule is newly written.

## The current game direction

The concept has passed through several perspectives. Early discussion included sides, cards, dice, and an election outcome. A later computer and phone version placed the player in the escaping opposition figure’s position, with NPCs helping or obstructing the journey. The origin report preserves those earlier ideas. [2]

The latest direction restores side choice and shifts attention toward the staff or team processing people. The opposition side helps a recognized target progress using bogus credentials. The winning side tries to stop the target while handling routine paperwork and other people. Successive stages carry the escape forward, with an airport encounter involving passports, visas, or departure near the end. [3]

Ron has not selected a points system. He is considering cards earned through skill and given by people the player processes, with benefits that could matter later. Opposing NPCs may interfere, but their behavior remains open. The computer and phone context continues; the latest revision does not itself establish a multiplayer format. [3]

The strongest development is that the player now performs the administrative work that gives the larger story its consequences. The escape can emerge through ordinary encounters. The target’s significance comes partly from appearing within a process the player already knows how to operate.

That gives routine work a narrative purpose. Other people establish what an ordinary encounter looks like. A consequential arrival can then change the meaning of familiar actions. How much routine is needed, and how it remains interesting, belongs to the developing experience.

## Using the blueprint to describe the invented system

The blueprint can describe how COALITION works on its own terms. It does not need to reconstruct the article. The author can begin with invented encounters and examine how they connect.

For each encounter, the blueprint can distinguish what the player sees, what other characters are doing, what the player can change, and what continues into the next scene. This exposes relationships between the storyline and the interaction without supplying a complete ruleset.

The following table follows Ron’s proposed interactions. These are places to examine, not a mandatory list of levels. Several could belong to one scene.

| Part of the concept | Visible player activity | Work or relationships behind it | Result or open choice |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Routine stream | Read paperwork and respond to people. | Fictional instructions, NPC circumstances, and the institution’s role. | Establishes ordinary work; competence and queue consequences remain open. |
| Target encounter | Recognize the target among the people being processed and respond toward the chosen side’s objective. | What the player has learned about the target, what other characters know, and what the credentials claim. | Recognition and the processing decision affect the journey; their exact demands remain open. |
| Processing result | Interpret what follows the response. | The fiction determines who acts on that response. | The person’s situation changes; the exact consequence remains authored. |
| Handoff | Engage with the next encounter. | Another character or office responds to earlier work. | Establishes what carries forward and why. |
| Later card use, if retained | Understand or use an earlier gift. | The NPC relationship gives the benefit meaning and reach. | Earning conditions, use, and effects remain exploratory. |
| Airport conclusion | Complete the final passport, visa, or departure encounter. | Whatever people and arrangements the invented ending needs. | Resolves the chosen story outcome; location and completion remain open. |

The visible activity may be a brief exchange at a desk while other characters act elsewhere. The blueprint can preserve that difference. The player does not need access to every cause for the story to make sense, but the author benefits from knowing why a later event follows.

The same approach can connect an ordinary queue, the target’s appearance, subsequent handoffs, and the final airport. It can also reveal when two scenes are repeating the same decision without adding anything. A new backdrop alone may provide atmosphere; a changed relationship between information, authority, and consequence can give the encounter a different meaning.

The journey can be broad or compressed. The player needs to understand an encounter’s result well enough to engage with what follows.

## Player perspective, knowledge, and decisions

Choosing a faction establishes the player’s desired outcome. It does not yet establish exactly whose work they perform. That distinction is useful because allegiance and practical control can create different experiences.

The player might identify with a particular employee, with a team, or with a side represented through several encounters. These remain possible readings of the current proposal. The story can establish whichever relationship Ron wants, including institutional powers invented specifically for the game.

The blueprint helps locate continuity. What persists across stages: the player character, the assignment, the target, the team, or the consequences of prior work? That question concerns how the player understands their place in the story. It does not require a realistic employment or travel explanation.

A second distinction concerns knowledge. Reading a document accurately, recognizing a person, and choosing an outcome helpful to a faction are separate tasks. The current proposal brings them together, but their relationship remains open.

Recognizing the target within ordinary traffic could itself be the central skill. Ron’s description makes that recognition part of the player’s job. The blueprint can establish what information makes it possible and how the player learns to use it. Once recognition happens, the response may be straightforward or may demand further judgment; either can fit the intended experience. This is a choice about where the challenge sits, rather than a requirement to add more mechanics.

The bogus credentials take their significance from the interaction. Recognizing a discrepancy, understanding a fictional rule, and deciding how to act on that knowledge can produce different experiences.

The immediate result also deserves its own place in the model. What the player intended, what their action changed, and what another character concludes need not be identical. The story can use those differences, provided the player has a way to make sense of them as the game develops.

## Ordinary people, helpers, and opposition

The queue provides more than background. It can establish the world through the people who need something from the institution and through the ordinary situations the player encounters. Those people need not all exist solely to advance or obstruct the target.

Their treatment matters to the possible card system. If someone later helps the player, the earlier interaction can give that assistance a narrative basis. The relationship becomes part of the continuing story rather than an unexplained reward.

A recurring helper could function as a patron, but that role remains available for invention. The person’s connection to the player and the kind of help they offer can be established through the fiction. A later benefit can be surprising while still making sense in retrospect.

Opposing NPCs have a similarly open role. The current proposal establishes that some characters may thwart progress without specifying how. Their actions could affect what the player knows, what they can accomplish, or what happens after a decision. These are aspects to evaluate, not selected behaviors.

The important distinction is between what the player can understand as opposition and what appears arbitrary. The author can conceal a motive or defer an explanation. The blueprint can still record how the character’s action connects to the situation, even when that connection remains hidden from the player.

## Skill, gifts, and the possible card system

The proposed cards give earlier encounters a possible effect on later play. Their narrative meaning comes before any need to select quantities, prices, or points.

A card might express a relationship or some other benefit established by the fiction. The report leaves that meaning open. The useful question is whether the player understands why the person provides it, what it changes, and why it can matter in a later encounter.

The blueprint can connect an NPC’s earlier encounter to the benefit they provide later. This gives the card a place in the continuing story.

The phrase “earned through skill” introduces an important question about what the game recognizes as competent play. Accurate processing, success for a faction, and satisfaction for the person being processed may point in different directions. Their relationship has not yet been decided.

If gifts follow approvals, blanket approval could become more rewarding than attentive work. It could also favor the facilitating side over the blocking side, even when both players act competently toward their selected objectives. These are possible incentive problems, not claims that the proposal already contains a flawed system.

If rewards instead arrive independently of performance, they may create surprise without expressing the intended skill. That could be a valid choice, but it would serve a different purpose. The current evaluation therefore leaves open how competence, character response, and a later benefit relate.

No fixed economy follows from having cards. Points, money, costs, timing, and reward schedules remain unselected. The earlier discussion should not be read as having settled them.

## The final airport

The final airport can be an existing Nelosphere location or a place invented for the ending. The journey can combine or omit travel stages to suit the game.

The blueprint’s question is what that encounter means within the story. What does completing it accomplish for the target and the chosen side? What does the player understand to be resolved?

The answer can be departure, arrival, escape, interception, or another outcome Ron establishes. A final encounter works as an endpoint when its significance follows from the game the player has experienced.

## Valdória and the Nelosphere

Valdória is a useful working inspiration within the Nelosphere. Existing material gives it an Atlantic core, a western corridor reaching the Pacific, and a multilingual setting. Its formal name is the Valdórian Citizens Republic of Edeland; Valdôria is the national Nelôxian form. [4, 5]

Existing names can provide texture if Ron wants them. Portô Venla and Venlasport are co-official names for the capital. Santa Laura and Litorāl offer other regional references. Verdenese, English, and recognized regional languages provide cultural variety. [4, 5]

Completely new cities, airports, jurisdictions, and institutions are equally available. The game can establish its own route and political crisis. Worldbuilding and prototyping can develop together.

Language variety can make the fictional documents and encounters feel situated. Different names or languages can be ordinary features of the world, with whatever significance the author assigns them. Existing currency or institutional references supply optional material without choosing the mechanics.

## Questions for the next evaluation

The most useful next questions concern the player’s experience:

- What does the player actually do during an ordinary encounter and during the target’s appearance?
- How does the player recognize the target, and how much of the challenge lies in recognition versus the subsequent response?
- Whose work does the player control, and what carries across handoffs?
- What changes because of a decision, and how does the player understand that change?
- What makes an NPC’s later help or opposition meaningful within the story?
- What does a card represent, and how does earning it relate to competent play on either side?
- What makes the airport encounter a satisfying completion of this fictional journey?

These questions keep the evaluation attached to the game Ron is creating. The blueprint is a tool for making its invented system legible and playable.
