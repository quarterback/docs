# From an ordinary system to a playable experience

**Interaction design game sprint: concept and rationale report**

Proposed by Ron Bronson  
Recorded October 29, 2026

## Purpose and current status

Ron is considering turning the following week’s interaction design lesson on data visualization into a weeklong sprint in which students build a game. His framing is: **“what boring thing can you turn into a game.”** Students would interpret a situation, storyboard the experience, write its storyline, and use an agent to implement a playable version. The process would follow the kind of reading and analysis that produced the COALITION concept from an article about an escape. [1]

Ron’s subsequent clarification establishes the central emphasis: **“the blueprint i did for the bolsanro thing is the real key.”** He described “a real heft to what has to happen here before they can get to the game making” and recognized that he needs to undertake that same work for his own game. [1]

This report preserves the proposal, its rationale, and questions to resolve when developing an assignment. Ron has proposed the teaching direction. The analysis below explains its possibilities and limits; suggested ways to examine student work remain considerations for later assignment design. Class size, student experience, assessment requirements, and the existing lesson’s precise objectives have not been established in this conversation.

## The process that prompted the teaching idea

The [COALITION origin and concept report](coalition-origin-and-concept-report.md) records the original article, Ron’s prompt, and the initial service-blueprint analysis. His interest centered on the practical dependencies involved in an escape: money and influence still leave a person needing transport, cooperation, processing, and a destination willing to receive them. The southward route through another country was the detail that made those dependencies especially interesting to him. [2]

The educational opportunity begins with that act of noticing. A student encounters an account of something happening and identifies a relationship, delay, restriction, or handoff that deserves closer attention. A possible game emerges through interpreting the situation. The source does not arrive with a complete set of mechanics attached.

“Boring” gives students a direction in which to look. Familiar activities such as course registration, arranging a repair, or coordinating an event can contain conflicting requirements and uneven access to information. These are illustrative possibilities raised in the discussion, rather than assigned subjects. The student’s task would be to identify what makes their chosen situation worth examining and whose experience they want to represent.

## The blueprint carries the analysis

The blueprint can map an observed process or an invented system. It connects the experience someone sees with the people, organizations, information, and work that make it possible. The distinction between visible interactions and supporting work is part of the service-blueprint approach described in the 18F method reference. [3]

For this proposed sprint, the following are useful areas of inquiry. They describe what a blueprint could reveal, rather than a required submission format.

| Area | What the analysis needs to make visible |
| --- | --- |
| Actors and objectives | Who is involved, what each participant wants, and who can affect another person’s progress. |
| Actions and handoffs | What happens, who does it, and what another person needs before they can act. |
| Visible interactions | What the person moving through the process can see, request, provide, or receive. |
| Supporting work | Coordination, decisions, records, and preparations outside that person’s immediate view. |
| Information and dependencies | Who knows what, which conditions must be satisfied, and where timing or sequence matters. |
| Evidence and open decisions | For an account of real events, what is established or inferred. For an invented system, what has been authored and what remains undecided. |

When a blueprint claims to explain actual events, its gaps and inferences matter. The original escape account left operational details unexplained. [2] A fictional game can freely invent those details or an entirely different process. Its blueprint can help the designer make the invented relationships coherent and decide what becomes playable.

This is part of the work Ron means by “heft.” Students would make purposeful choices about the system they want to represent or create. The blueprint can expose a research question, an unresolved fictional rule, a dependency that matters more than expected, or a premise that overlooks the most consequential part of the process.

## From the blueprint to story and interaction

The blueprint and the game serve different purposes. The blueprint examines the process across participants. The game selects an experience within that process and gives the player particular abilities, knowledge, and objectives. Choosing that perspective is a design decision.

The storyboard would then connect the storyline to actions. At a particular moment, the player has a goal, encounters information, makes a choice, and receives a response. That response changes what they can understand or do next. A sequence of story events alone may leave these interactions unspecified.

The useful question is what the player can change at each moment and how they recognize the consequence. The student may discover that an apparently dramatic event offers little participation, or that a modest decision becomes interesting because of its dependencies. Different readings of the same source can produce different games without one automatically being the correct adaptation.

Nicky Case’s account of making explorable explanations offers a relevant reference: begin with a question that creates curiosity, give the audience a concrete experience, and build understanding through connected consequences. That is a practitioner’s approach students could examine, rather than a formula this proposal requires them to follow. [4]

## Student authorship and the agent’s role

Ron explicitly envisages students using an agent to build their games while authoring the storyboard and storyline themselves. The blueprint adds the prior account of how the situation works. Together, these materials give students something against which to judge an implementation. [1]

An agent may propose mechanics, fill gaps, or produce a polished interface. Students would still need to decide whether those additions express their interpretation. The COALITION conversation demonstrated this issue: Ron repeatedly identified when suggestions had drifted toward a different game. Later, he independently reconsidered a paperwork mechanic and gave it a specific role in his own concept. The chronology matters because the same broad reference can be an unwanted substitution at one point and a deliberate authorial choice later. [1, 2]

A useful record could therefore show which assumptions the student retained, revised, or rejected, and why. The purpose would be to make their decisions inspectable. The quantity of code generated would reveal little about whether they understood the source or designed an intelligible interaction.

## Where data visualization fits

The proposed sprint has a strong potential relationship to information design. Players need to understand conditions, compare options, recognize constraints, and interpret consequences. Visual representations can make those relationships available for action.

A queue, timeline, map, or record of changing quantities might serve that purpose, depending on the chosen situation. Their presence alone would not establish that students had met a particular data-visualization objective. The relevant work includes deciding what information matters, how it is encoded, what comparisons are possible, and what a player might misunderstand.

The Pudding’s [lawn-mowing interactive](https://pudding.cool/2026/06/mow/) provides a concrete reference. It offers a playable mowing task and then examines participants’ routes and behavior through visual analysis. It demonstrates a connection among ordinary activity, interaction, and data. Its production scale and research effort are not proposed expectations for a one-week student project. [5]

One useful distinction is that **representing information and deciding how a simulated system behaves are different design tasks.** A fictional processing delay can be authored for play. If someone presents it as a measured fact about a real process, that empirical claim needs evidence.

If the existing lesson specifically teaches empirical datasets, quantitative comparison, or visual encoding, those objectives would need an intentional place in the eventual assignment. Fictional game data can also be represented clearly. The conversation has not yet established how much of the original lesson the sprint would replace or what source requirements students would have.

## COALITION as a possible instructor example

The companion [blueprint and checkpoint concept report](coalition-blueprint-and-checkpoint-concept-report.md) records the latest game direction, its inspiration in the article, and possibilities within the Nelosphere.

Ron’s latest game idea gives him a way to undertake the process he is considering for students. He is exploring levels built around participation in an escape: a player aligned with the opposition helps a targeted figure progress, while a player aligned with the winning side tries to prevent the departure. Routine processing and recognizing the relevant person would give the larger operation a specific interaction. [1]

The exact documents, level boundaries, player role across locations, and relationship between paperwork and recognition remain open. Ron has also raised the possibility that people who pass through could provide cards useful later, while NPCs interfere. He has not settled a points system or an economy. These possibilities belong in a developing concept record, with their uncertainty preserved.

Ron clarified that his game can use entirely fictional details, including new Nelosphere cities. Rules, routes, actors, and political events can all be invented. The article helped him notice interesting dependencies; it does not require a faithful reconstruction or constrain him to existing world details. His blueprint can establish how that fictional system works. As an instructor example, the revealing material would be how those choices become playable. This clarification concerns Ron’s game; the class assignment’s approach to fiction remains open. [1]

## What could be revisited when writing the assignment

The conversation suggests examining the relationship among the source, blueprint, storyboard, playable experience, and revision. These are possible forms of evidence for critique, not an adopted rubric. Watching a classmate play could reveal whether the player understands a dependency, sees the available choices, or attributes an outcome to the wrong cause.

For a weeklong scope, the earlier discussion favored a small experience that can be completed and revised. The amount of preliminary analysis and the feasible implementation scope still need to be considered together. The presence of an agent does not determine how much a student can investigate, interpret, test, and improve within the available time.

Questions left open for assignment development include:

- Which existing interaction-design and data-visualization objectives must the sprint address?
- What kinds of sources are suitable, and what additional inquiry is feasible when an account has gaps?
- What student experience, tool access, and individual or team arrangements can be assumed?
- What should be ready before implementation begins, and what can continue to change through making?
- How will critique distinguish the student’s interpretation, visual communication, and revision from the polish of generated output?

Ron’s central premise remains the basis for those decisions: substantial interpretation must precede the game. The eventual assignment can make that work visible, and his own development of COALITION can provide a concrete account of what it involves.
